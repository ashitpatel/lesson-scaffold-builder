The [last post]({{ site.baseurl }}/what-it-takes-out/) covered the deletions. This one covers the additions: fifteen small teaching methods, each allowed at most twice per unit, each with strict conditions — and a standing permission to use none of them.

## Memory and practice: five techniques with trial support

These are the only additions with broad, replicated evidence about learning. Each works offline and fits inside an existing step. Each has **non-negotiables** — the parts the evidence is about. A version that can't include all of them is hollow, and the right answer is to abstain. Full citations: [references](https://github.com/ashitpatel/lesson-scaffold-builder#references).

- **Recall from memory.** Open a later lesson with a few questions on earlier content, answered with notes away, then show the answers so each learner checks their own. Nothing collected or marked. Rated high utility in a major review of learning techniques ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)); backed by a meta-analysis of testing versus restudy ([Rowland, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)) and the study that made the testing effect famous ([Karpicke & Roediger, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408)).
- **Spaced review.** Return to earlier content at least a week later, with learners doing something — answering, solving, explaining — not just looking. Rated high utility ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)) and the subject of a large quantitative synthesis ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)).
- **Worked examples first.** Show a complete example step by step, then one for learners to finish, before they solve alone. A recommendation of the IES practice guide ([Pashler et al., 2007](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/1)), a Rosenshine principle ([Rosenshine, 2012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ971753)), and a research review in its own right ([Atkinson et al., 2000](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ627412)).
- **Check every learner.** Ask a question the whole class answers at once — slates, fingers, scrap paper — look at every answer, and re-explain if many are wrong before moving on. A Rosenshine principle ([Rosenshine, 2012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ971753)) in the tradition of the formative-assessment review ([Black & Wiliam, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1177/003172171009200119)) — with one honest caution: a later meta-analysis across 42 effect sizes found a weighted mean of 0.20, real but modest ([Kingston & Nash, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2011.00220.x)).
- **Show it with the words.** Put the picture or model next to the words or symbols, and state the link between them. Two IES practice-guide recommendations: combine graphics with verbal descriptions, and connect abstract and concrete representations ([Pashler et al., 2007](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/1)).

That evidence comes mostly from laboratories and classrooms in high-income countries. It supports the techniques as generally effective; **it doesn't predict an effect size for your lesson, school or system**, and no card may claim one.

## Peers and interest: five designs for engagement

These come from Connected Learning research, which is mostly ethnographic and design-based: it describes how engagement happens but **doesn't establish effects on test scores**, and no card claims one.

- **A choice of theme.** Same practice, same check — but learners pick the theme or the format, a calculation on sports scores or on crop growth.
- **Rotating peer roles.** Instead of one child recording alone, named roles — Measurer, Recorder, Checker — that rotate. Never a fixed leader.
- **Everyday numbers.** Replace textbook-only figures with quantities the class can observe at school. Never household or family details.
- **A class product.** A class table or class book alongside individual work, with no ranking.
- **A real audience.** Finished work shown to someone beyond the teacher, at school, only by the learner's choice.

## Class votes: five formats behind a strict gate

Class votes apply **only where there is a real resource trade-off for the class to decide** — more options than fit, or competing proposals where only some can win. Never in decoding drills, never in the teacher's own admin, never for decisions that aren't the class's. Where the gate passes:

- **A mock budget ledger** for arithmetic practice: pairs pick options whose made-up costs fit a made-up cap, and say what they gave up.
- **A decision cycle** for governance lessons: ideas, vetting against stated criteria, ballot, report-back.
- **Comparing counts**: approval voting against single-choice; proportional counting in higher grades.
- **A shared-resource round** for a real class resource: a garden bed, materials, time slots.
- **A report-back**: the result and the counting rule, on chart paper.

Like the peer designs, these are engagement and civic-reasoning designs, not levers on scores.

## The most common answer is no

Per unit, at most two cards and one added step — and "doesn't apply" is normal. A phonics drill gets no vote. A lecture with no matching step gets no memory technique it can't support. An excluded family never appears. Every change is tagged on the card (e.g. `[changed: EB-04]`) with a "what changed and why" note, so a teacher can see exactly which method touched which step — or confirm that none did.
