# Evidence-based practices: what they are, when they fit, and what supports them

LSB applies nine teaching practices, and only these nine. This page explains them for teachers and school leaders: what each one is, the classroom situation it fits, and the research behind it. The pipeline reads the short, strict version in [evidence-based-practices.md](../frameworks/evidence-based-practices.md); full citations live in the [README references](../README.md#references).

The nine fall into five groups: two that strengthen memory, two that explain clearly, two that make learners think harder, one that designs practice better, and two that meet learners where they are.

## 1. Strengthening memory

### EB-01 Retrieval practice

**What it is.** Learners answer questions from memory — then check their own answers. Typically a few short questions opening a later lesson, on scrap paper, slates or aloud, with the answers shown straight after so each learner marks their own. Nothing is collected or graded.

**When it fits.** When learners must remember facts, terms, methods or steps for later work, and nothing currently asks them to recall it from memory. It replaces re-reading or copying, never teaching: showing the answers afterwards is part of the method, not optional.

**Evidence.** Practice testing earned one of only two high-utility ratings in a major review of ten learning techniques ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)). Behind that rating sit a meta-analysis of testing versus restudy ([Rowland, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)), the *Science* study that made the testing effect famous ([Karpicke & Roediger, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408)), and a strong-evidence recommendation in the IES practice guide ([Pashler et al., 2007](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/1)).

### EB-02 Spaced practice

**What it is.** Returning to earlier content in a later week, with learners doing something — answering, solving, explaining — not just looking at it again.

**When it fits.** When key content is practised only in the week it is taught but later units or the final task need it. The review must come at least a week after first teaching; a reminder the next day is not spacing.

**Evidence.** The review's other high-utility rating ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)), a large quantitative synthesis of distributed practice ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)), and an IES recommendation ([Pashler et al., 2007](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/1)).

## 2. Explaining clearly

### EB-03 Worked examples, then practice

**What it is.** Show a complete example worked step by step, then one with the last step left for learners to finish, before they solve similar problems alone. Alternating works too: one example, one similar problem, repeat.

**When it fits.** When learners first meet a new procedure — a calculation, a method, a problem type — and would otherwise go straight to solving alone, or only ever watch. Not for open-ended creative or argument writing, where a full example would hand over the answer.

**Evidence.** An IES recommendation ([Pashler et al., 2007](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/1)), one of Rosenshine's principles ([Rosenshine, 2012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ971753)), and a review devoted to the worked-examples literature ([Atkinson et al., 2000](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ627412)).

### EB-05 Concrete and visual representations

**What it is.** A drawing, model or demonstration shown *together with* the words or symbols, with the link between them stated out loud — not the picture instead of the explanation.

**When it fits.** When an idea is carried only by abstract words or symbols and learners have nothing to hold onto. The representation must use materials already in the classroom.

**Evidence.** Two IES practice-guide recommendations: combine graphics with verbal descriptions, and connect abstract and concrete representations ([Pashler et al., 2007](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/1)).

## 3. Thinking harder

### EB-08 Self-explanation

**What it is.** After each step of a worked example, learners say or write *why* the step works — to a neighbour, in one line, or aloud to themselves — before solving alone. The correct reasoning is confirmed straight after.

**When it fits.** When learners only read, watch or copy examples without processing them. The prompts must ask for reasoning (why this step), not recall of facts, and must target content already taught.

**Evidence.** A moderate-utility rating ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)) and the study that named the effect, on students explaining worked examples while learning to solve problems ([Chi et al., 1989](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1)).

### EB-09 Elaborative interrogation

**What it is.** Turn telling into asking-then-telling: "Why does X happen?" or "How does X cause Y?" Learners attempt an answer from the lesson, then the educator states the precise answer.

**When it fits.** Facts, definitions or cause-effect statements learners must remember, where a why/how question has a precise answer in the lesson. Vague "discuss" prompts and questions without lesson answers don't count.

**Evidence.** A moderate-utility rating ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)) and the flagship study on learning facts from paragraphs ([Woloshyn et al., 1990](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.82.3.513)) — run with adults, so the card restricts it to precise questions with stated answers rather than open discussion.

## 4. Designing practice

### EB-07 Interleaved practice

**What it is.** Mix practice items across already-taught types instead of drilling one type in a block — same items, shuffled — with a "which method fits this one?" choice before each item.

**When it fits.** Practice sets where each type has already been taught and practised once in blocked form. Interleaving is for consolidating choice between methods, never for first teaching: each type starts blocked, then mixes.

**Evidence.** A moderate-utility rating ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)) and the study that brought shuffling into mathematics practice ([Rohrer & Taylor, 2007](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2008-00329-002)).

## 5. Meeting learners where they are

### EB-04 Checking for understanding

**What it is.** During the lesson, ask one or two questions that *every* learner answers at the same time — slates held up, fingers for a choice, scrap paper — look at all the answers, and re-explain if many are wrong before moving on.

**When it fits.** When the educator explains or models something new and would otherwise move on without seeing whether every learner followed. Volunteers answering is the failure mode this replaces.

**Evidence.** A Rosenshine principle ([Rosenshine, 2012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ971753)) in the tradition of the formative-assessment review ([Black & Wiliam, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1177/003172171009200119)) — with one honest caution: a later meta-analysis across 42 effect sizes found a weighted mean of 0.20, real but modest ([Kingston & Nash, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2011.00220.x)).

### EB-06 Teaching at the right level (level groups)

**What it is.** A quick unmarked check places learners in two or three fluid groups by current level, and practice steps give each group the same skill at its level in the same minutes. Groups are re-checked as learners progress; nothing is permanent, labelled or ranked.

**When it fits.** Foundational practice — reading, mathematics — where learners span widely different levels and one-size practice strands the bottom while boring the top. It needs no extra periods: existing practice time is regrouped, not extended.

**Evidence.** Randomized trials of remedial level-group teaching in India ([Banerjee et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.122.3.1235)) and randomized evaluations of Teaching at the Right Level at scale, including a dedicated-hour school model ([Banerjee et al., 2016](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/22746.html)). One caution: the trials used dedicated instructors and time; the card adapts the level-grouping idea to ordinary classroom practice, which is a smaller claim.

## Why these nine, and not others

The memory pair are the Dunlosky review's only two high-utility techniques; the thinking pair and interleaving are its moderate-rated techniques, now included. The explaining pair and the checking practice come from the IES guide, Rosenshine's principles and the formative-assessment literature; level grouping comes from the TaRL trials. Left out are the review's low-rated techniques (highlighting, rereading, summarising, among others). All nine share two properties: each works offline with paper or chalk, and each fits classroom teaching — all but EB-06 inside a single step, EB-06 across a unit's existing practice time.

## What the evidence does and doesn't say

These syntheses and trials mostly draw on laboratory studies, high-income classrooms, or — for TaRL — Indian primary schools. They support the practices as generally effective; **they don't predict an effect size for a given lesson, school or system**, and no step on a card may claim one. The practice list itself is a project decision that still needs review by the people who own the content — see the [selection note](../frameworks/evidence-based-practices.md).

**Where the cards sit in the wider evidence.** Cost-effectiveness reviews for low- and middle-income countries rate *structured pedagogy* and *teaching by learning level* among the best buys for learning. Structured pedagogy there means a package: lesson plans, learning materials and ongoing support for teachers. LSB's cards are only the lesson-plan part, so that evidence can't be claimed for the cards alone. Whether the cards work, and at what cost, is a separate question that only a field evaluation can answer: see the [evaluation protocol](evaluation-protocol.md#who-builds-this-kind-of-evidence-and-how-lsb-users-can-draw-on-it).
