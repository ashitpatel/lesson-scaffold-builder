==============================================================================
LESSON SCAFFOLD: Unit 5 - Ecosystems: Energy Flow and Food Webs
==============================================================================
Goal: Students explain how energy flows through an ecosystem and predict how a
change to one population affects the others.
Grade band: Middle. Period: 45 minutes.

UNIT                  WHAT IT IS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
MLS-ECO-01            Sorting the Living Things on the School Grounds (2
                      weeks). Starts fresh (no earlier unit needed).
MLS-ECO-02            Food Webs and Energy Pyramids (3 weeks). Builds on
                      MLS-ECO-01.
MLS-ECO-03            Quadrat Sampling of the School Grounds (4 weeks). Builds
                      on MLS-ECO-01.
MLS-ECO-04            What If a Species Disappeared? (2 weeks). Builds on
                      MLS-ECO-02, MLS-ECO-03.

Units that build on the same earlier unit, or start fresh, can run at the same
time.

The LSB framework functions purely as an adaptive, supportive scaffolding
framework for lesson content delivery. It does not prescribe operational
classroom routines, behavior management habits, or physical school rules.


==============================================================================
MLS-ECO-01: Sorting the Living Things on the School Grounds
2 weeks. Starts fresh (no earlier unit needed).
==============================================================================
Goal: Gives the class a shared list of school-grounds organisms, sorted by how
each one gets its energy, which is where tracing energy flow starts.

WHO                   WHAT TO DO
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Lead Educator      Explain briefly that an ecosystem is a community of
                      living things together with the non-living parts of
                      their environment, such as water, soil, air and
                      sunlight, all interacting as a system. Write this
                      definition and the words biotic (living) and abiotic
                      (non-living) on the board. Students copy them into their
                      notebooks.

2. Lead Educator      Explain that nearly all energy in an ecosystem comes
                      from the sun. Define producers (plants and algae that
                      capture sunlight through photosynthesis), consumers
                      (herbivores, carnivores and omnivores) and decomposers
                      (fungi, bacteria, many worms and insects that break down
                      dead material and return nutrients to the soil). Write
                      the three groups as column headings on chart paper.

3. Lead Educator      Before going outside, name three familiar organisms,
[changed: EB-04]      such as grass, a goat and a mushroom, one at a time. For
                      each, every student holds up one, two or three fingers
                      at the same moment for producer, consumer or decomposer.
                      Look at all the hands, and re-explain any group that
                      many students get wrong before moving on. No names or
                      answers are recorded.

4. Lead Educator      Take the whole class onto the school grounds together
                      and stay with all the groups. In small groups, students
                      list in their notebooks every organism they can find and
                      sort each one as a producer, consumer or decomposer.
                      Students only look: they do not touch or pick up
                      animals, insects, fungi or plants, and do not lift
                      stones or logs.

5. Lead Educator      Back in class, combine the group lists into a single
                      class list on the chart paper, under the producer,
                      consumer and decomposer headings. Keep the chart,
                      because the next units use it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BEFORE YOU START
- Needs: A dry day for the school-grounds survey.
  If not: Teach step 2 first and do the survey on the next dry day in the same
  two weeks. If no dry day comes, groups list the organisms they can see
  through the classroom windows, then do step 4.
- Needs: The season leaves enough organisms visible on the grounds.
  If not: Groups also list organisms they know live on the grounds but cannot
  see that day, mark them with a question mark, and sort them in the same way.
- Needs: Chart paper is available for the class list.
  If not: Use the back of a reused poster or flattened cardboard. Or write the
  list on the board and have students copy it into their notebooks.
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CHANGES FROM THE SOURCE
- Evidence-Based, EB-04 Checking for Understanding: after the three groups are
  explained, every student answers three classification questions at once with
  fingers, and the educator looks at all the hands and re-explains if many are
  wrong before the fieldwork; nothing is recorded


==============================================================================
MLS-ECO-02: Food Webs and Energy Pyramids
3 weeks. Builds on MLS-ECO-01.
==============================================================================
Goal: Builds the class food web and the energy pyramid that students use to
explain how energy passes, and is lost, from producers up to top consumers.

WHO                   WHAT TO DO
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Lead Educator      Recall from MLS-ECO-01: use the class chart of
                      producers, consumers and decomposers. Explain that a
                      food chain shows one path energy takes, for example
                      grass → grasshopper → frog → snake. Explain that most
                      animals eat more than one food and are eaten by more
                      than one predator, and a food web shows all these
                      feeding relationships together.

2. Lead Educator      Give each student a card, made from reused card or
[changed: CL-02]      paper, naming one organism from the class list. Students
                      stand in a circle and pass a ball of string from each
                      organism to the things that eat it, until the string
                      forms a web. As they pass the string, each student
                      writes on the back of their card the names of the
                      organisms they passed it to.

3. Lead Educator      Ask one student to let go of their string. Lead a class
                      discussion about which organisms are affected when that
                      species disappears.

4. Lead Educator      Students read out the links from their cards in turn
[changed: CL-02]      while two students at the chart paper build the class
                      food web: one draws an arrow from each organism to each
                      thing that eats it, and the other checks it against the
                      card. After three links they swap roles, and after three
                      more they hand over to the next two students in the
                      circle. Check each arrow as it is drawn. Keep this chart
                      for the final unit.

5. Lead Educator      Explain that at each step of a food chain most energy is
                      used for living (moving, growing, keeping warm) or lost
                      as heat, and only about a tenth is stored in the bodies
                      at the next level. Explain that this is why food chains
                      rarely have more than four or five links, and why there
                      are far fewer large predators than plants.

6. Lead Educator      Work one food chain from the class food web on the board
[changed: EB-03]      step by step, saying each step aloud: start with 10,000
                      energy units at the producer, divide by ten for each
                      level, choose a scale so the producer bar fills the
                      board's width, and draw each level as a bar. Then write
                      a second chain with its last level left for students to
                      calculate and draw in their notebooks, and show the
                      answer.

7. Lead Educator      Give a full lesson for this work. Each student takes one
                      food chain from the class food web, starts with 10,000
                      energy units at the producer level and calculates the
                      energy at each level. On squared paper, students choose
                      a scale so the producer bar fills the paper's width, and
                      draw each level as a bar to that scale. Any level
                      shorter than one square is drawn as a single thin line
                      with its energy value written beside it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BEFORE YOU START
- Needs: A ball of string is available for the web.
  If not: Tie together strips of scrap cloth or paper, or reused yarn, to make
  a long cord.
- Needs: There is room in the classroom for the whole class to stand in a
  circle.
  If not: Move the desks to the walls, or use a larger indoor room such as a
  hall, with the whole class and the educator together.
- Needs: Squared paper is available for the energy pyramids.
  If not: Students rule a grid of 1 cm squares on plain or reused paper, or in
  their notebooks.
- Needs: All three weeks of class time are available.
  If not: If a week is lost, do steps 1 and 2 in one period and steps 3 and 4
  in the next. Keep the full lesson for step 6.
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CHANGES FROM THE SOURCE
- Evidence-Based, EB-03 Worked Examples, then Practice: one complete worked
  energy-pyramid example on the board, then a second with its last level left
  for students to finish, before each student calculates and draws their own
  pyramid in step 7 (unchanged, originally step 6)
- Connected Learning, CL-02 Structural Peer Roles: as they pass the string,
  each student writes on the back of their card the organisms they passed it
  to, so the links read out and checked in step 4 are on the cards
- Connected Learning, CL-02 Structural Peer Roles: rotating student pairs draw
  the class food web instead of the teacher alone: one draws, one checks
  against the card, then they swap and hand over; the educator still checks
  each arrow


==============================================================================
MLS-ECO-03: Quadrat Sampling of the School Grounds
4 weeks. Builds on MLS-ECO-01.
==============================================================================
Goal: Produces the class's own counts of plants and animals, the evidence
students use to predict how losing one population would affect the others.

WHO                   WHAT TO DO
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Lead Educator      Recall from MLS-ECO-01: groups will use the class list
[changed: EB-05]      of school-grounds organisms to name what they find.
                      Explain that scientists cannot count every organism, so
                      they sample. A quadrat is a square frame, usually one
                      metre on each side. Draw the school grounds on the board
                      as a rectangle with four small squares in it, and
                      explain that counts from these quadrats estimate what is
                      in the whole rectangle.

2. Lead Educator      Before going outside, ask one question every student
[changed: EB-04]      answers at once with a thumb up or down: if one quadrat
                      has five snails, are there exactly five snails on the
                      whole school grounds? Look at all the thumbs, and if
                      many are up, re-explain with the board drawing before
                      moving on. No answers are recorded or named.

3. Lead Educator      Take the whole class onto the school grounds together
                      and stay with all the groups. Each group marks out a
                      one-metre quadrat at a fixed spot using four sticks and
                      string. Students do not touch or pick up animals,
                      insects, fungi or plants inside the square, and do not
                      lift stones or logs.

4. Lead Educator      Once a week for four weeks, take the whole class to the
                      quadrats together and stay with all the groups. Each
                      group tallies the plants and animals inside its quadrat
                      in a notebook, using the class list to name them, and
                      group members take turns as recorder. Students count by
                      looking only: they do not touch, pick up or move any
                      organism, or lift stones or logs.

5. Lead Educator      After the last visit, each group compares its own weekly
                      tallies. Copy each group's totals into one class table
                      on chart paper, one column per quadrat, and the class
                      compares results across quadrats. Keep the table for the
                      final unit.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BEFORE YOU START
- Needs: Weather allows a visit to the quadrats in each of the four weeks.
  If not: Move the visit to another dry day in the same week. If a whole week
  is lost, make three visits instead of four and do step 4 in the same week as
  the last visit.
- Needs: The quadrats stay in place at their fixed spots between visits.
  If not: Leave only the corner sticks in place and re-tie the string at each
  visit. If sticks go missing, mark the square out again at the same spot
  using landmarks noted in the group's notebook.
- Needs: Sticks and string are available.
  If not: Use fallen twigs for the corners, or lay reused string or cloth
  strips in a one-metre square on the ground.
- Needs: Groups can name what they find using the class list (no field guides
  are bought).
  If not: Groups tally an unfamiliar organism under a short description, such
  as 'small brown beetle', and use that same description at every visit.
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CHANGES FROM THE SOURCE
- Evidence-Based, EB-05 Concrete and Visual Representations: the spoken
  explanation of sampling now comes with a board drawing of the grounds as a
  rectangle with four quadrat squares, and the step says how the squares'
  counts stand for the whole area
- Evidence-Based, EB-04 Checking for Understanding: a thumbs up or down
  question every student answers at once on whether one quadrat's count is the
  total for the grounds. The educator looks at all the answers and re-explains
  with the drawing if many are wrong. Nothing is recorded


==============================================================================
MLS-ECO-04: What If a Species Disappeared?
2 weeks. Builds on MLS-ECO-02, MLS-ECO-03.
==============================================================================
Goal: Each student uses the class food web and quadrat evidence to predict, in
a revised written explanation, how losing one organism would affect the
school-grounds ecosystem.

WHO                   WHAT TO DO
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Lead Educator      Recall from MLS-ECO-02: the class food web chart and
                      what happened when one string was let go. Recall from
                      MLS-ECO-03: the class quadrat table. Explain that humans
                      change ecosystems by clearing land, introducing new
                      species, using pesticides and changing the climate,
                      which can remove species from food webs or add species
                      that compete with or eat those already there.

2. Lead Educator      Name one plant from the class food web. Every student
[changed: EB-04]      writes on scrap paper one organism that would lose food
                      without it and holds it up at the same moment; look at
                      all the papers and re-explain if many are wrong before
                      moving on. No names or answers are recorded.

3. Lead Educator      Students read two short case studies. One is about a
                      species introduced to an island that had no predators
                      for it. The other is about a wetland restored after
                      years of drainage.

4. Peer Facilitator   In small mixed groups, students discuss how each change
                      in the case studies moved through the food web. A
                      different student leads the discussion of each case
                      study, so no one leads both, and everyone in the group
                      speaks before the group moves to the next case study.

5. Lead Educator      Each student chooses one organism from the class food
                      web chart. Give two full lessons of quiet writing time
                      for each student to draft, in their own words, a
                      two-page explanation of what would happen to the
                      school-grounds ecosystem if that organism disappeared.
                      It traces effects through at least three feeding
                      relationships, uses evidence from the class quadrat
                      table and considers at least one uncertain effect.

6. Peer Facilitator   In pairs, students swap drafts and each one reads their
                      partner's explanation.

7. Lead Educator      Give a full lesson for each student to revise their own
                      explanation after reading their partner's draft, and to
                      write a final copy.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BEFORE YOU START
- Needs: Printed copies of the two case studies are available for students.
  If not: Read each case study aloud while students listen, or copy a short
  summary of each onto the board.
- Needs: MLS-ECO-03 finished all four weekly visits.
  If not: Students use the tallies from the visits that did take place (for
  example three) as their quadrat evidence.
- Needs: Both weeks of class time are available.
  If not: If time is lost, read only one case study and hold the group
  discussion in the same period. Keep the full writing and revision lessons.
- Needs: There is paper for two-page drafts and final copies.
  If not: Students write in their notebooks or on the backs of reused paper.
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CHANGES FROM THE SOURCE
- Evidence-Based, EB-04 Checking for Understanding: a separate check after the
  explanation: every student writes on scrap paper one organism that loses
  food without a plant the educator names and holds it up at the same moment;
  the educator looks at all the papers and re-explains if many are wrong;
  nothing is recorded
