# Grade 9 Environmental Studies — Pond Management

*Class 6 to 9, mainly rural schools. Adapted from Step 3, Activity A of the DRCSC lesson plans.*

## 1. The ponds of our area
A pond is a very common feature of our area. The class revisits the water source maps from the earlier mapping activity and discusses the ponds: why are there so many ponds here, and why do some areas have only a few? Do family members use a pond every day, and for what: bathing, washing clothes and utensils, swimming, fishing, watering gardens, duckery?

## 2. Songs and sayings about ponds
Children collect poems, songs and popular sayings that refer to a pond. If pond references are fewer than river or sea references, the class tries to figure out why. The collection is shared and discussed for what it says about how the community values ponds.

## 3. Sketching the pond
Each child studies the pond nearest their home: its location (near houses, among rice fields, by the roadside), its size and shape, the trees and plants around and on it, and the condition of the bank — covered with plants, brick, concrete, or eroded. Sketches are done individually or in groups.

## 4. Users, interviews and observations
Children find out who uses the pond and how: how many people, for what purposes, with what other materials (soap, detergent, fish feed, irrigation pump), and how usage varies with the seasons. They check hygiene and sanitation around the pond: dumped garbage, plastics, human or animal waste, sewage inflow. They note animals, birds and insects on and around the pond, ask about the pond's history, judge whether the water is dirtier than earlier and how the quantity changes with the seasons, and record any social function or religious activities such as pujas held at the pond.

## 5. Sharing and analysing
The class pools all findings into charts, graphs and diagrams and debates: are there community rules for pond use? Are there typical pond designs, and what makes a well-designed pond? What pollutes pond water — chemical fertilisers, pesticides, detergent, oil, organic waste — and what follows? What can protect the pond and improve its surroundings? Is it true that the pond causes too many mosquitoes?

## 6. A pond design and action plan
Children draw up a pond design and management plan to recommend to the community, following the example of a well-managed village pond in South 24 Parganas: pond banks planted with multi-purpose trees, medicinal plants, vegetables, taro and ipomoea; a soak pit for soap and detergent water; space kept for people's use. The class implements what it can — clearing garbage near ponds, planting the banks, making awareness posters — and writes a report of the activity, what is expected to follow, and sends it to the ENRE network to share with other schools.

## 7. Ideas for town schools
Town children make a guide map of pond, lake and water-related parks in their town with observation notes on trees, birds and creatures; interview senior community members for a historical map of ponds and marshland reclaimed for houses and factories; and survey trash dumping around town ponds to plan an improvement action.
